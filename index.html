What is the Law of Large Numbers?

Imagine flipping a fair coin. Any single flip is unpredictable — you might get several heads in a row purely by chance. But if you flip that same coin thousands of times, the proportion of heads settles down and gets very close to 50%. That settling-down behavior is the Law of Large Numbers (LLN).

Law of Large Numbers (informal statement): As the number of independent trials of a random experiment increases, the sample mean (the average of the observed outcomes) converges to the experiment's theoretical mean — also called the expected value.

It does not say that random outcomes "even out" in the short run (that misconception is called the gambler's fallacy). A coin has no memory — after 10 heads in a row, the next flip is still 50/50. What the LLN guarantees is that any short-run streak gets diluted and washed out as more and more trials pile up.

The math behind it

For a random variable with possible values x and probabilities P(x), the theoretical (expected) mean is:

μ = Σ [ x · P(x) ]

After n trials with observed outcomes x₁, x₂, …, xₙ, the sample mean is:

x̄ₙ = ( x₁ + x₂ + … + xₙ ) / n

The Law of Large Numbers says: as n → ∞, x̄ₙ → μ. In practice, we can't run infinitely many trials — but the more trials we run, the closer we typically get, and the less our average bounces around.

A quick example

A fair six-sided die has outcomes 1 through 6, each equally likely (probability 1/6). Its theoretical mean is:

μ = (1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5 + 6) / 6 = 3.5

If you roll it only 5 times, your average might be 2.4 or 4.8 — pure luck plays a big role with so few trials. But roll it 5,000 times, and your average will almost certainly land very close to 3.5.

Why it matters

The Law of Large Numbers is the reason insurance companies can price policies, casinos stay profitable over millions of bets even while individual gamblers win big, pollsters trust large survey samples more than small ones, and quality-control inspectors can estimate a factory's defect rate from a large batch of products. Small samples are noisy; large samples are trustworthy.
